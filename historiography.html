What Weber claimed, and did not

Weber’s thesis is often stated more strongly than he stated it. He argued for what later translators call an elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft; Parsons renders it “correlations”) between a religious ethic and an economic attitude: each suited and reinforced the other. He explicitly disclaimed any wish “to substitute for a one-sided materialistic an equally one-sided spiritualistic causal interpretation of culture and of history.” His object was the spirit of capitalism, the sense that methodical acquisition is a moral duty, not capitalist institutions as such, which he knew were older.

The essay depends on only the narrowest part of this: that Reformed theology taught a practical syllogism, that believers were urged to seek assurance through a disciplined life, and that the resulting conduct outlived its rationale. The broader economic thesis is set out below with its main critics.

The main lines of criticism

1. Weber’s opening statistics

Weber opened with figures from his student Martin Offenbacher’s 1900 study of Baden, showing Protestants overrepresented in higher schooling and business. Kurt Samuelsson (Religion and Economic Action, 1957; English 1961) noticed that one row Weber reproduced summed to 109 percent. Once each school was compared with its own district’s population, he found “no differences worth mentioning.” Later reanalyses (Richard Hamilton, 1996; George Becker, 1997) reduced the gap Weber reported.

What survives: Weber’s argument never rested mainly on these figures, but its empirical opening is weak.

2. Capitalism came first

Banking, double-entry bookkeeping, bills of exchange, and long-distance commercial partnerships flourished in Catholic northern Italy and Flanders long before the Reformation. Samuelsson argued further that Puritan teaching was not distinctively pro-capitalist and that Catholic moralists also preached diligence and thrift.

What survives: Weber conceded the institutional point. His claim concerns a widely held ethos, which these facts narrow but do not refute.

3. Influence ran both ways

R. H. Tawney (Religion and the Rise of Capitalism, 1926) accepted a connection but argued that commercial classes also shaped Puritan teaching, which adapted over the seventeenth century to accommodate trade.

What survives: A mutual relationship, compatible with “elective affinity” but not with religion as prime mover.

4. Literacy, not anxiety

Sascha Becker and Ludger Woessmann (2009) used county data from 1870s Prussia, with distance to Wittenberg as an instrument for Protestant adoption. Protestant areas were more prosperous, but higher literacy, a by-product of Luther’s insistence on Bible reading, accounts for most of the gap.

What survives: A real Protestant economic effect, through a different mechanism than Weber proposed.

5. No growth effect

Davide Cantoni (2015) examined 272 German cities from 1300 to 1900 and found no effect of Protestantism on city growth, with estimates precise enough to rule out sizable effects.

What survives: Little of the strong economic claim for Germany. The cultural and psychological account is untouched by this test.

6. Values without wages

Jörg Spenkuch (2017), studying contemporary Germany, found no effect of Protestantism on hourly wages, but found that Protestants work longer hours, a difference explained mostly by values.

What survives: A measurable, value-driven difference in work effort, the closest modern evidence for a persisting “ethic.”

7. Other routes to modernity

Robert Bellah’s Tokugawa Religion (1957) found a functional analogue of the Protestant ethic in Tokugawa Japan, especially in Ishida Baigan’s Shingaku teaching of diligence and frugality, though later scholars dispute parts of his reading. Deirdre McCloskey (Bourgeois Dignity, 2010) attributes modern growth to a change in the rhetoric of commercial dignity. Joseph Henrich (The WEIRDest People in the World, 2020) traces Western individualism to the medieval Church’s dissolution of kin-based institutions.

What survives: Protestantism as one route among several to a disciplined, work-centered ethos. That is consistent with the essay’s narrower focus on one lineage.

A different kind of objection: subtraction stories

Charles Taylor (A Secular Age, 2007) objects not to Weber’s economics but to a common way of narrating secularization: as the removal of religious belief, leaving behind a natural human residue. Taylor argues that modern secularity was constructed, with new moral orders, new social imaginaries, and new conceptions of the self. The essay adopts this objection by treating each era’s major premise as an authority newly constructed, not as what remained after the previous one was subtracted.

Where this leaves the essay

The economic thesis is contested and probably overstated. The cultural and theological account, that Reformed teaching produced a practical syllogism and a methodical way of life, is well documented in the primary sources. The essay rests on the second and claims nothing about the first.
