EXHIBIT A

Benjamin Franklin

Weber chose Franklin as his main example on purpose. Franklin grew up in a strict Puritan household in Boston, and as a teenager, by his own account, he “soon became a thorough Deist.” He believed in a Creator and in providence, and thought “the most acceptable service of God was the doing good to man.” Near the end of his life he wrote that Jesus’s moral system was “the best the world ever saw,” while admitting “some doubts as to his divinity.”

So Franklin wasn’t an atheist, but the Calvinist apparatus was gone: no double decree, no anxious search for signs. What remained were the virtues, justified by their results. His list of thirteen (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, humility) came with a daily chart for marking his lapses. It was Puritan self-examination, recast as self-improvement.

Remember, that time is money. He that can earn ten shillings a day by his labour, and goes abroad, or sits idle, one half of that day, though he spends but sixpence during his diversion or idleness, ought not to reckon that only expense; he has really spent, or rather thrown away, five shillings besides.

Remember, that money is of the prolific, generating nature. Money can beget money, and its offspring can beget more, and so on.
Benjamin Franklin, Advice to a Young Tradesman (1748)

Two Puritan ideas survive without God. Idle time is a loss, not a neutral pause, which is Baxter’s “deadliest of sins” turned into accounting. And money exists to be put back to work, not enjoyed. The language is commercial, but the moral pressure is the same.

The summum bonum of this ethic, the earning of more and more money, combined with the strict avoidance of all spontaneous enjoyment of life, is above all completely devoid of any eudæmonistic, not to say hedonistic, admixture. It is thought of so purely as an end in itself, that from the point of view of the happiness of, or utility to, the single individual, it appears entirely transcendental and absolutely irrational.
Weber on Franklin, The Protestant Ethic, ch. 2

This is Weber’s key observation. Franklin’s ethic isn’t about getting rich in order to enjoy life. Making money methodically is a duty, almost a sacred one, even though the sacred reason for it is gone. From a purely self-interested point of view that is strange, and Weber calls it “irrational.” It only makes sense as something left over from religion.

INTERACTIVE

Same behavior, different motive

The behaviors barely change across three centuries. The reasons people give for them change completely. Switch the framing to see it.

Six habits, three framings

Pick a framing. The behaviors on the left stay the same.

Working long, methodical hours

Labor in a calling is commanded by God, and a diligent, fruitful life may be a sign that I am among the elect.

Industry is a virtue. It makes a man useful, independent, and respectable, and idleness leads to ruin.

My work expresses who I am. I do it because I want what I’m building, not because I owe anyone.

Saving instead of spending

Luxury endangers the soul. Wealth is held in trust for God, not for my comfort.

Frugality builds credit, independence, and character. Waste is a moral failure.

Saving is strategy, a way to buy freedom and fund future goals. It isn’t a moral test.

Reinvesting profit

A good steward doesn’t bury his talents. Refusing a lawful gain is refusing God’s purpose.

Money begets money. Leaving it idle throws away everything it could have produced.

Growth is the point. The enterprise itself is the achievement, and scaling it is the game.

Accounting for every hour

Wasted time is the deadliest sin. Every hour belongs to God and must be used for His glory.

Time is money. An idle half-day costs what you spent plus what you didn’t earn.

Time is the raw material of my life. I optimize it for what I value.

Feeling uneasy when idle

Idleness may be a symptom that grace is absent. The unease is a warning about my soul.

Idleness is a vice and the road to poverty and dependence. The unease is conscience.

Rest is healthy, even necessary. And yet the unease is still there, and it’s hard to say why. This is the residue.

Reading success as meaningful

Fruitfulness in my calling is a possible sign of God’s favor.

Prosperity is evidence of good character: diligence rewarded.

Success measures competence and value. It reflects who I am.

The fifth row is the one Weber and Nietzsche both point at. Once a feeling outlasts the reason for it, people keep having it without being able to explain it.

THE AMERICAN VERSION

From Puritan to self-made man

In nineteenth-century industrial America the ethic became a national story, mostly cut loose from its theology but still carrying moral weight.

  • The self-made man. Frederick Douglass gave his lecture “Self-Made Men” many times from 1859 onward, praising people who rose by their own effort. The phrase became a central American ideal.
  • The success story. Horatio Alger’s novels, beginning with Ragged Dick (1868), popularized the rags-to-respectability plot. Read closely, his heroes usually rise through luck and a wealthy patron as much as through effort, but the culture remembered the effort.
  • Wealth as stewardship. Andrew Carnegie’s essay “The Gospel of Wealth” (1889) argued that the rich should give away their fortunes during their lifetimes: “The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced.” Baxter’s steward is still there, now without Calvin’s God.

By the twentieth century the result was a culture where hard work and self-reliance carried moral prestige for people of every religion and none. Many Americans who have never heard of Calvin still feel that being busy means being good.

In Baxter’s view the care for external goods should only lie on the shoulders of the “saint like a light cloak, which can be thrown aside at any moment.” But fate decreed that the cloak should become an iron cage.
Weber, The Protestant Ethic, ch. 5

This is where the famous “iron cage” comes from. Weber’s German was stahlhartes Gehäuse, roughly “a shell as hard as steel”; Talcott Parsons translated it as “iron cage” in 1930. For the Puritan, wealth was supposed to be something you could shrug off. Once the economic machine was built, nobody could shrug it off. People work methodically now not to prove their election but because the system requires it.
