Several thinkers have argued the result is endless argument, then group loyalty, then new sacred objects. ---
THE GRAMMAR

Fragments of older traditions

The philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre opens After Virtue (1981) with a thought experiment. Imagine a catastrophe destroys science. Centuries later, people find fragments, such as half-burned pages and bits of theory, and start using the words again: “neutrino,” “mass,” “specific gravity.” They argue fluently, but the words no longer connect to the practice that gave them meaning.

MacIntyre’s claim is that our moral language is in that state. We still use words like “virtue,” “duty,” “rights,” and “justice,” but they come from different traditions (Aristotelian, Christian, Enlightenment) that once had coherent foundations. With the foundations gone, the words survive as fragments, and people who use the same word often mean incompatible things.

The most striking feature of contemporary moral utterance is that so much of it is used to express disagreements; and the most striking feature of the debates in which these disagreements are expressed is their interminable character.
Alasdair MacIntyre, After Virtue (1981), ch. 2

The disagreements are interminable because they can’t be settled: each side reasons validly from premises the other side doesn’t share, and there’s no common standard to weigh the premises against. When argument can’t settle things, something else will, usually which side you belong to.

WEBER AGAIN

The return of the old gods

Weber saw a version of this coming. In his 1917 lecture “Science as a Vocation” he described modernity as the “disenchantment of the world” and predicted that values would not vanish but multiply and compete:

Many old gods ascend from their graves; they are disenchanted and hence take the form of impersonal forces. They strive to gain power over our lives and again they resume their eternal struggle with one another.
Weber, “Science as a Vocation” (1917; trans. Gerth & Mills)

Weber’s “polytheism of values.” Without a single overarching God, people are left with competing ultimate commitments (nation, class, art, science, pleasure, justice) and no higher authority to decide between them. Each person, Weber says, has to choose which god to serve.

THE SEQUENCE

A hypothesis in five stages

Put these thinkers together and you get a possible sequence. It’s a model to test against history, not a law.

Stage 1

Shared sacred order

One framework is taken for granted. Morality and meaning come bundled together. Taylor, Durkheim

Stage 2

Secular residue

Belief thins but habits and vocabulary persist: work ethic, duty, dignity. Weber, Nietzsche

Stage 3

Fragmentation

Moral words remain but rest on incompatible foundations. Arguments can’t be settled. MacIntyre, Rieff

Stage 4

Tribalization

Without a shared standard, belonging decides. Disagreement turns into identity. Haidt

Stage 5

Re-sacralization

Religious energy attaches to ideas, leaders, or identities. Salvation through the right side. Voegelin, Tillich

Why groups re-sacralize

The sociologist Émile Durkheim (The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, 1912) argued that the sacred is partly a social product. Groups hold together by setting certain things apart as untouchable, then gathering around them. If he’s right, a society doesn’t stop producing sacred things when it stops believing in God. It produces them wherever groups form.

The psychologist Jonathan Haidt (The Righteous Mind, 2012) puts a modern spin on this: morality “binds and blinds.” Shared sacred values bind a group together, and the same values make it hard to see the other side as reasonable. When a society has one broad shared story, binding happens at the level of the whole society. When the story fractures, it happens at the level of smaller tribes.

Combine that with Tillich’s point that everyone has an ultimate concern, and you get the stage-five pattern: political causes, public figures, and identities start to carry religious weight. They get treated as sources of salvation, objects of devotion, and lines that can’t be crossed.

Limits of the model

  • Religious societies were tribal too. Europe’s Wars of Religion were fought between people who shared a God. A common faith didn’t prevent tribal violence; it often organized it.
  • Secular societies can be cohesive. Several highly secular countries have high social trust, stable politics, and strong work norms.
  • It can explain too much. If every strong commitment counts as a substitute religion, the idea stops distinguishing anything.
  • Causes are tangled. Media, economic change, and technology all fragment shared narratives, and secularization may be as much an effect as a cause.

The value of the model is the questions it raises: what holds a society together, what happens to religious impulses when religion recedes, and whether a moral vocabulary can survive the loss of its foundations. Readers can judge how well it fits the present.
