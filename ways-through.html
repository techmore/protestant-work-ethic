THE PROBLEM

Two bad exits

Parts I and II leave two obvious ways out, and both fail.

Go back. Restore the old shared story by force or nostalgia. But a faith imposed for its social usefulness isn’t the faith that originally held things together, and people can tell the difference.

Find a new savior. Put the full religious weight on a political cause, a leader, a nation, or an identity. This is what Voegelin, Tillich, and the whole twentieth century warn against. When a finite thing is treated as ultimate, the people who stand in its way start to look like obstacles to salvation.

Most of the serious answers lie between these. They are local, limited, and slow, which is part of why they’re trustworthy.

THE IRONY AT THE START

The Reformation began with the answer

The deepest twist in this whole story: the Reformation started as an escape from anxious striving. Luther’s breakthrough was that a person’s standing before God is a gift, received by faith, not a reward earned by performance. You can’t earn it, and you don’t have to.

Within a few generations, Calvinist uncertainty about who had received the gift turned that relief into the most relentless striving in history. A movement that began by saying “you can’t earn it” produced people who couldn’t stop trying to prove it.

So the first way through is the oldest one, rediscovered.

SEVEN WAYS THROUGH

What serious people proposed

Way 1 · Assurance

You are accepted

Answers: “Am I okay?”

The theologian Paul Tillich, who also warned about idolatry, preached a sermon in 1948 that goes straight at the search for signs. His claim is that the sense of being okay can’t be produced by evidence you collect. It can only be received.

You are accepted. You are accepted, accepted by that which is greater than you, and the name of which you do not know. Do not ask for the name now; perhaps you will find it later. Do not try to do anything now; perhaps later you will do much. Do not seek for anything; do not perform anything; do not intend anything. Simply accept the fact that you are accepted!
Paul Tillich, “You Are Accepted,” in The Shaking of the Foundations (1948)

This is Luther’s grace in a form addressed to modern people, including those unsure about God. Notice the order: acceptance comes first and work comes after (“perhaps later you will do much”). That’s the reverse of the Puritan sequence, where work came first and anxiously awaited a verdict.

Way 2 · Rest

Leisure is not laziness

Answers: “Why do I feel guilty when idle?”

Two books from the years just after World War II make the case that rest is not a break from what matters but the place where what matters happens. The Catholic philosopher Josef Pieper warned against a world of “total work,” where every human activity is justified only by its usefulness. The rabbi Abraham Joshua Heschel described the Sabbath as a weekly refusal to be defined by production.

Judaism is a religion of time aiming at the sanctification of time… The Sabbaths are our great cathedrals.
Abraham Joshua Heschel, The Sabbath (1951)

The direct counter to Baxter’s “waste of time is the deadliest of sins.” For Heschel, some time is meant to be useless in the economic sense, and that is what makes it holy. The secular version is simpler: a life with no protected time from output belongs entirely to output.

Way 3 · Meaning

Meaning without a master plan

Answers: “What’s it all for, if not salvation?”

Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist who survived Auschwitz, argued that meaning is found rather than manufactured: in work worth doing, in love for particular people, and in the stance a person takes toward suffering they can’t avoid. Albert Camus, an unbeliever, rejected both religious and political salvation and argued for revolt with limits. His book The Rebel is a sustained attack on revolutions that kill in the name of a future paradise.

Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one’s attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one’s own way.
Viktor Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning (1946)

Frankl doesn’t promise that history will turn out right or that you’ll be proven worthy. He locates meaning in something no outcome can remove. That makes it immune to the search for signs.

I rebel—therefore we exist.
Albert Camus, The Rebel (1951)

Camus’s point is that real rebellion against injustice affirms a shared human dignity, and so it carries its own limit. Once it starts sacrificing actual people for an abstract future, it has betrayed the solidarity it began with.

Way 4 · The line through the heart

Refusing the tribal map

Answers: “Who are the bad guys?”

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn spent eight years in the Soviet labor camps, a system built by people certain they were on the right side of history. His conclusion is the simplest antidote to tribal salvation there is.

If only it were all so simple! If only there were evil people somewhere insidiously committing evil deeds, and it were necessary only to separate them from the rest of us and destroy them. But the line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being. And who is willing to destroy a piece of his own heart?
Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, The Gulag Archipelago (1973)

Every political religion draws the line between groups: the elect and the damned, us and them. Solzhenitsyn moves it inside each person. That doesn’t erase real injustice, but it makes it impossible to locate all evil in your opponents and all goodness in yourself.

Way 5 · Small scale

Living in truth, locally

Answers: “What can one person actually do?”

Václav Havel, a dissident playwright who later became president of Czechoslovakia, wrote “The Power of the Powerless” (1978) about a greengrocer who puts a party slogan in his shop window because everyone does. Havel’s proposal was to stop: refuse to participate in what you know is false, in your own small sphere. He called this “living within the truth.”

Alasdair MacIntyre, whose After Virtue diagnosed the fracture in Part II, ends the book on a similar note. A shared moral grammar can’t be rebuilt nationally by decree. It can be rebuilt in small communities that practice the virtues together and keep them alive through hard times.

We are waiting not for a Godot, but for another—doubtless very different—St. Benedict.
Alasdair MacIntyre, After Virtue (1981), final sentence

St. Benedict didn’t save Rome. He founded small communities with a rule of life (the Benedictine motto is ora et labora, pray and work) that preserved learning through centuries of collapse. MacIntyre’s hope is about faithful practice at small scale, not a mass movement.

Way 6 · Forgiveness and responsibility

Starting again without starting over

Answers: “How do we live with what’s already been done?”

Hannah Arendt, a Jewish refugee from Nazi Germany, argued that human action can’t be undone and has consequences no one can fully foresee. Without some way to release each other from past acts, every group is trapped in endless retaliation. Her answer was forgiveness, which she treated as a political capacity, not only a private virtue.

The possible redemption from the predicament of irreversibility—of being unable to undo what one has done though one did not, and could not, have known what he was doing—is the faculty of forgiving.
Hannah Arendt, The Human Condition (1958)

“Redemption” in the most down-to-earth sense: not the transformation of history, but the ability of people to release each other and begin again.

Weber himself, at the end of his life, offered the counterpart. In “Politics as a Vocation” (1919) he contrasted an ethic of pure conviction, which does the righteous thing whatever the consequences, with an ethic of responsibility, which owns the consequences. His picture of real change is unglamorous:

Politics is a strong and slow boring of hard boards. It takes both passion and perspective.
Max Weber, “Politics as a Vocation” (1919; trans. Gerth & Mills)

Way 7 · Tending

Tend something alive

Answers: “Where do I start, tomorrow morning?”

The smallest and most concrete way through. The Garden and the Hearth describes what the device paradigm removed: daily obligation to living things, and practices that gather people. Albert Borgmann’s answer is to deliberately keep a few focal practices at the center of life, like the shared meal, the garden, and the craft, and let devices serve them rather than replace them.

The Kentucky farmer and poet Wendell Berry spent sixty years living this way on purpose, and his poem “Manifesto: The Mad Farmer Liberation Front” reads like a rule of life for it: “Plant sequoias.” “Every day do something / that won’t compute.” It ends with two words:

Practice resurrection.
Wendell Berry, “Manifesto: The Mad Farmer Liberation Front” (1973)

Something that died can be brought back through practice, not only belief. That’s the lesson of the whole story in reverse. The Puritans’ habits outlived their faith; for people starting from the other end, habits of tending may be where a deeper foundation starts to grow back. A plant on the windowsill is a small thing, but it is something alive that depends on you, on a schedule you don’t control.

A TEST

Sane redemption or new idol?

Taken together, these thinkers suggest seven questions to ask of any movement, leader, ideology, or personal program that promises to make things whole, whether it’s religious, political, or self-help.

  1. Where does it draw the line between good and evil?Between groups, or through every heart, including the hearts of its own members? (Solzhenitsyn)
  2. Does it promise salvation through history?Will everything be fixed once the right side wins? (Voegelin, Camus)
  3. Does it make something finite ultimate?A nation, leader, identity, or cause treated as beyond question. (Tillich)
  4. Does it let you rest?Or does it demand total mobilization and constant proof of loyalty? (Pieper, Heschel)
  5. Does it have room for forgiveness?Can opponents and failures ever be released, or only punished? (Arendt)
  6. Does it work at the scale of real life?Family, neighbors, craft, and community, or only abstractions? (Havel, MacIntyre)
  7. Does it ask you to tend anything?Does it give you something alive, a person, a place, a practice, to care for daily, or only things to consume and opinions to hold? (Borgmann, Berry)

None of the thinkers above passes every test perfectly, and they disagree sharply about God. But they agree about the shape of a sane answer: received rather than earned, limited rather than total, local rather than messianic, and able to forgive.

The story isn’t finished. Part I was written by Calvinists, merchants, and philosophers. Part II is the world you woke up in. Part III is still being written, by everyone who notices the story and decides how to live in it.
