THE THREAD

One question, many answers

Go back to Part I. The Puritan couldn’t know whether they were elect, so they looked for signs, and a disciplined, fruitful life became the sign they could check. The work was never really the point. Assurance was.

Follow that need for assurance, rather than the work, through the centuries:

EraThe questionWhere people looked for signsWhat failure meant
Puritan, 1600sAm I elect?Fruitfulness in a calling; a methodical lifePossible damnation
Franklin, 1700sAm I virtuous?A daily chart of thirteen virtues; credit and reputationBad character
Self-made man, 1800sHave I made something of myself?Rising from nothing; wealth; independenceWeakness, dependence
Organization man, 1950sDo I belong and advance?Title, salary, the house, the promotionBeing left behind
NowAm I enough?Productivity, metrics, visibility, being on the right sideNot counting, not mattering

The question gets vaguer as it goes. “Am I elect?” at least had a definite meaning. “Am I enough?” has no clear standard, so no amount of evidence ever settles it. The Puritan’s uncertainty had an answer, even if only God knew it. Ours may not have one at all.

THE ACHIEVEMENT-SUBJECT

The boss moved inside

The Korean-German philosopher Byung-Chul Han (The Burnout Society, 2010) argues that the dominant pressure today isn’t external discipline but self-imposed achievement. Nobody forbids you to rest; you just can’t. The old disciplinary society said “you must.” Achievement society says “you can,” and “you can” turns out to be harder to escape, because there’s no one to rebel against.

The exploiter is simultaneously the exploited.
Byung-Chul Han, The Burnout Society

This is Weber’s iron cage with the bars moved inward. The Puritan at least knew who was watching. The achievement-subject watches themselves, with a standard that has no ceiling. Han reads burnout and depression as what happens to a self that is asked to keep proving itself with no endpoint.

Small signs of this are everywhere once you look. A 2017 study in the Journal of Consumer Research (Bellezza, Paharia & Keinan) found that Americans increasingly read busyness itself as a status symbol, a sign that you’re in demand and valuable. Leisure used to signal status. Now being too busy for it does. It’s Baxter’s “waste of time is the deadliest sin,” without Baxter’s God.

COLLECTIVE SIGNS

Being on the right side

The search for signs also runs at the group level. If, as Voegelin argued (see Substitutes), modern movements move salvation into history, then being on “the right side of history” becomes a kind of election, and every news cycle is checked for evidence.

This shows up across the political spectrum, and it has the same structure as the Puritan’s anxiety. You can’t be sure you’re among the saved, so you look for signs: the right opinions, publicly displayed, and the right enemies, clearly denounced. As with the Puritan, the uncertainty never resolves, so the vigilance never ends.

None of this means work, achievement, or political conviction are bad. The point is narrower. When any of them is asked to answer “Am I okay?”, it gets loaded with a weight it was never built to carry, and people start to feel that weight as anxiety.

YOU ARE HERE

The middle of the story

Here’s where Parts I and II leave you. You inherited a feeling of unease when idle, a sense that output equals worth, and a hunger for signs. You probably never chose any of these, and they came with no explanation. The theology that once explained them is gone for most people. The newer explanations (virtue, self-making, achievement, history) each work for a while and then stop working.

Seeing this doesn’t fix anything. But it changes your position. You stop being someone pushed around by an unexplained feeling and become someone who knows where the feeling came from, which means you can ask whether to keep it.

One more loss belongs in this picture before Part III: the household itself, which used to be full of living things to tend. That’s The Garden and the Hearth. Then the question of Part III: if the old answer is gone and the new answers keep failing, what have serious people proposed instead, without falling into a new fanaticism?
