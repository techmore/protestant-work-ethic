Primary sources

Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of CapitalismStart here
1905 / 1920

Shorter than its reputation suggests, and the actual argument about the calling, predestination, and the iron cage is subtler than any summary.

Max Weber, “Science as a Vocation”
1917

A lecture you can read in an hour, with the “disenchantment of the world” and the old gods rising from their graves.

Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography
1771–90

The secularized ethic in its own cheerful voice, virtue chart included. Read it next to Weber’s chapter 2.

Richard Baxter, A Christian Directory (selections)
1673

The Puritan pastor Weber leaned on most. Skim the sections on calling and time to hear the original pressure firsthand.

Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science and Twilight of the Idols
1882 / 1889

Read §125 and §343 of the first and the George Eliot passage of the second for the clearest statement of what “God is dead” means.

Ayn Rand, Atlas Shrugged and The Virtue of Selfishness
1957 / 1964

The heroic, atheist version of the productive ethic, worth reading as a primary source to see exactly where it departs from the Puritans.

The debate over Weber

R. H. Tawney, Religion and the Rise of Capitalism
1926

The classic sympathetic critique, showing religion and commerce reshaping each other over two centuries.

Becker & Woessmann, “Was Weber Wrong?” (Quarterly Journal of Economics)
2009

A readable economics paper that keeps the Protestant advantage but explains it through literacy.

Deirdre McCloskey, Bourgeois Dignity
2010

An energetic argument that what changed was how society talked about and respected commerce, not theology or institutions.

Joseph Henrich, The WEIRDest People in the World
2020

A sweeping, data-heavy account of how Western psychology became individualistic, starting centuries before Luther.

Secularization

Tom Holland, Dominion
2019

The most readable book on this list, arguing that even secular Western values are Christian at the root.

Charles Taylor, A Secular Age
2007

The definitive account of how belief went from unavoidable to optional. Very long, so dip in by chapter.

Émile Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life
1912

The foundational argument that societies generate the sacred, which is why it doesn’t disappear when belief does.

John Gray, Seven Types of Atheism
2018

A short, sharp tour of how many forms of unbelief quietly carry Christian hopes forward.

Substitutes and new ethics

Paul Tillich, Dynamics of Faith
1957

A short book that defines faith as “ultimate concern” and idolatry as treating something finite as ultimate.

Eric Voegelin, The New Science of Politics
1952

The dense but precise case that modern ideologies try to bring salvation into history.

Philip Rieff, The Triumph of the Therapeutic
1966

A strange, prophetic book on the shift from cultures of renunciation to cultures of personal well-being.

Daniel Bell, The Cultural Contradictions of Capitalism
1976

Argues capitalism eats its own Protestant foundations by producing a consumer culture that rewards gratification over discipline.

Tara Isabella Burton, Strange Rites
2020

A field guide to the spiritual lives of the religiously unaffiliated, from wellness to fandom to politics.

Fracture and community

Alasdair MacIntyre, After Virtue
1981

Explains why modern moral arguments never end: we’re using fragments of traditions whose foundations are gone.

Jonathan Haidt, The Righteous Mind
2012

The psychology of why morality binds groups together and blinds them to each other.

Christopher Lasch, The Culture of Narcissism
1979

What happens to the self when older disciplines, the work ethic included, lose their authority.

Robert Bellah et al., Habits of the Heart
1985

Interviews with Americans struggling to explain their commitments in anything but the language of individual choice.

Robert Putnam, Bowling Alone
2000

The data on the decline of American civic and religious participation, and what it did to social trust.
