THE DOCTRINE

Predestination

Luther had already made ordinary work sacred. A cobbler or a farmer serving faithfully in their Beruf (calling) was doing God’s work, no less than a monk. Calvin added something much harder.

John Calvin’s doctrine of predestination (the “double decree”) held that God, before creating the world, had already chosen who would be saved, the elect, and who would be damned. The Westminster Confession of 1646, the standard statement of English-speaking Calvinism, put it formally: some are “predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death.”

For the believer, three things followed:

  1. Nothing you do changes the decision. Good works don’t earn salvation.
  2. No church, sacrament, or priest can help. Calvinism removed the Catholic means of grace, such as confession and absolution.
  3. Officially, you can’t be certain which group you’re in.
In its extreme inhumanity this doctrine must above all have had one consequence for the life of a generation which surrendered to its magnificent consistency. That was a feeling of unprecedented inner loneliness of the single individual.
Weber, The Protestant Ethic, ch. 4

This is the emotional starting point. The most important question of your existence, heaven or hell for eternity, was already settled, and you had to face it alone with no way to find out the answer. Weber thinks this pressure, not the doctrine itself, is what changed behavior.

THE MOVE

From cause to sign

Pastors couldn’t leave their congregations in that state, and the advice they gave pointed in two directions. Believers had a duty to consider themselves chosen, because doubt was itself a temptation. And the best way to drive out doubt was intense, worldly activity in a calling.

Reformed theologians had a name for the reasoning people used, the practical syllogism (syllogismus practicus): the elect bear good fruit; my life bears good fruit; so I am likely elect. Work didn’t cause salvation. It was read as a sign of it.

What the theology saidWorks cannot save you. Election is God’s free and hidden decision. Certainty is not available.
What the psychology producedA disciplined, fruitful life became the main evidence people could check. Doubt drove more diligence.

The full sequence

PredestinationGod has already decided who is saved. You can’t change it, and you can’t be sure of it.
Uncertainty“Am I one of the elect?” No ritual or priest can answer, and the stakes are eternal.
Search for signsA disciplined, fruitful life in your calling becomes the evidence you can check. Not the cause of grace, but a sign of it.
Methodical work and thriftIdleness is a symptom of lacking grace. Luxury tempts the soul. Time must be accounted for. Life becomes systematic.

THE PASTOR

What this sounded like from the pulpit

Weber’s richest source isn’t Calvin himself but the English Puritan minister Richard Baxter, whose Christian Directory (1673) was practical advice on how to live. Baxter is clear that wealth for its own sake is dangerous. But he also treats passing up a legitimate opportunity to earn as a failure of stewardship:

If God show you a way in which you may lawfully get more than in another way (without wrong to your soul or to any other), if you refuse this, and choose the less gainful way, you cross one of the ends of your calling, and you refuse to be God’s steward.
Richard Baxter, Christian Directory (1673), as quoted by Weber, ch. 5

Notice the balance. Profit is not the goal, and enjoying it is suspect. But failing to make it is disobedience. Combine an obligation to earn with a prohibition on spending, and capital piles up almost automatically.

Weber summarizes Baxter’s attitude to time even more sharply: wasting time becomes “the first and in principle the deadliest of sins,” because every hour lost is an hour taken from labor for the glory of God.

The common misreading is “if you knew you were saved, you’d work hard out of gratitude.” Weber’s point is the reverse. It was the inability to know that drove the work. Certainty would have relaxed the pressure, and uncertainty kept it on.
