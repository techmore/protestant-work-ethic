THE DIAGNOSIS

Nietzsche: “God is dead”

The phrase is usually quoted as a boast. In context it’s closer to an alarm. In The Gay Science (1882), a madman runs into a marketplace looking for God, and the people there, who already don’t believe, laugh at him.

“Whither is God?” he cried; “I will tell you. We have killed him—you and I. All of us are his murderers… God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him. How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?… Is not the greatness of this deed too great for us? Must we ourselves not become gods simply to appear worthy of it?”
Nietzsche, The Gay Science §125, “The Madman” (trans. Kaufmann)

Nietzsche’s claim is cultural, not a proof that God doesn’t exist. Educated Europe no longer lived by Christian belief, but it hadn’t faced what that meant: Christianity had been the foundation for meaning, morality, and truth itself. The madman ends by saying he has come too early, because the event “is still on its way.” Its consequences hadn’t arrived yet. The last question, about having to become gods ourselves, anticipates the rest of this page.

They are rid of the Christian God and now believe all the more firmly that they must cling to Christian morality… When one gives up the Christian faith, one pulls the right to Christian morality out from under one’s feet.
Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols (1889), “Skirmishes of an Untimely Man” §5, on George Eliot

This is the same pattern as the secularized work ethic. The habits and values keep going after the belief that grounded them is gone. Nietzsche thought this couldn’t last: eventually people would ask why they held these values, and find no answer.

THE NEW ETHICS

From duty to self

One way to track what replaced the old ethic is to ask what people say work is for. Over three centuries the answer moves from God, to virtue, to the self, in two quite different forms.

The egoistic version: Ayn Rand

Rand, a committed atheist, kept the old ethic’s reverence for productive work and dropped both the duty and the self-denial. Her heroes work because creating is the highest expression of their own lives. Her “trader principle” says to deal with others only by exchanging value for value, never through force or pity.

Productive work is the central purpose of a rational man’s life, the central value that integrates and determines the hierarchy of all his other values.
Ayn Rand, “The Objectivist Ethics” (1961), in The Virtue of Selfishness

On the surface, this sounds like Baxter. The difference is in what it serves. For the Puritan, work served God and wealth was a dangerous trust. For Rand, work serves the worker’s own life, and enjoying its rewards is a virtue rather than a temptation. Same reverence, opposite justification.

The therapeutic version: Philip Rieff

The sociologist Philip Rieff traced a different successor. As older moral authority faded, Western culture moved from asking “Is this right?” toward asking “Is this good for me? Does it help me feel whole?” Work becomes fulfillment, authenticity, and self-actualization rather than obligation.

Religious man was born to be saved; psychological man is born to be pleased.
Philip Rieff, The Triumph of the Therapeutic (1966)

Rieff wasn’t celebrating. He thought a culture organized around personal well-being has trouble asking anyone to renounce anything, and that every previous culture had depended on shared renunciation.

POLITICAL RELIGIONS

Salvation moves into history

A third substitute is collective rather than individual. Several twentieth-century thinkers argued that the major ideologies of their era had the structure of religions: a fallen present, a coming redemption, an elect, enemies, and a path to salvation.

The political philosopher Eric Voegelin (The New Science of Politics, 1952) called this the “immanentization of the eschaton.” Christianity placed final redemption beyond history, at the end of time and in God’s hands. Modern mass movements tried to achieve it inside history through political action. (The phrase later became a conservative slogan, “Don’t immanentize the eschaton!”)

The theologian Paul Tillich gave a vocabulary for why this happens. He defined faith as “the state of being ultimately concerned.” On that definition, nobody lacks faith. Everyone treats something as ultimate. When the object is finite (a nation, a party, a leader, a cause) and is treated as if it were infinite, Tillich called the result idolatry.

Notice the parallel with the Calvinist’s anxiety. If salvation depends on history turning out right, then political opponents stop being people you disagree with and become obstacles to redemption, and compromise starts to feel like betrayal. That leads into the next page.
