--- layout: default title: Criticisms kicker: 05 · Criticisms & limits heading: Was Weber right? dek: The thesis has been contested for more than a century. Here are the strongest objections, and what is left standing after them. ---

Weber himself was careful. He said he did not want to replace “a one-sided materialistic” explanation with “an equally one-sided spiritualistic causal interpretation.” His claim was about an elective affinity: a religious ethic and an economic form that suited each other and reinforced each other. Most critics attack stronger versions of the thesis than the one Weber stated. Even so, the objections are serious.

1. Capitalism came first

Banking, double-entry bookkeeping, bills of exchange, joint-stock ventures, and long-distance trade flourished in Catholic northern Italy and Flanders centuries before the Reformation. Florence, Venice, Genoa, and Antwerp were capitalist hubs while Luther was still a child.

What survives: Weber agreed that capitalist institutions came earlier. He was explaining a spirit: the attitude that methodical acquisition is a moral duty, held widely by an entire class. The critique still narrows how much his thesis can explain.

2. The causation runs both ways

R. H. Tawney (Religion and the Rise of Capitalism, 1926) accepted a link but argued it ran in both directions. Commercial classes were drawn to theologies that suited them, and Puritan teaching itself bent over the seventeenth century to accommodate commercial life.

What survives: A mutual relationship rather than a one-way cause. That’s compatible with “elective affinity,” but it weakens any claim that religion came first.

3. It was literacy, not anxiety

Economists Sascha Becker and Ludger Woessmann (“Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History,” 2009) used county data from nineteenth-century Prussia. Protestant areas really were more prosperous, but the gap is largely explained by higher literacy. Luther wanted every believer to read scripture, which meant schools, which meant human capital.

What survives: The correlation is real, and it does come from religion. The mechanism may be education rather than salvation anxiety.

4. The growth effect may not exist

Davide Cantoni (“The Economic Effects of the Protestant Reformation,” 2015) compared hundreds of cities in the German lands from 1300 to 1900 and found no measurable effect of Protestantism on population growth, a standard proxy for economic development. Earlier, Kurt Samuelsson (Religion and Economic Action, 1957) attacked the statistics Weber started from.

What survives: Little of the strong economic claim, at least for Germany. The cultural and psychological description may still hold even where the growth effect doesn’t.

5. Other causes matter more

Many historians emphasize property rights, legal institutions, Atlantic trade, coal, and competition between states. Deirdre McCloskey (Bourgeois Dignity, 2010) argues the key change was in rhetoric: society began to respect merchants and innovators. Joseph Henrich (The WEIRDest People in the World, 2020) looks further back, to the medieval Church dissolving kin-based clans and producing more individualistic people long before Protestantism.

What survives: Religion as one cultural input among many, rather than the main engine.

6. Modern data is mixed

In rich countries today, differences in work attitudes between Protestants and Catholics tend to be small and inconsistent. Some micro-level studies, for example Jörg Spenkuch’s work on contemporary Germany (2017), find Protestants work somewhat longer hours. Others find little difference once income, education, and region are controlled for.

What survives: This is roughly what Weber’s own ending predicts. Once the ethic secularized and spread through the whole culture, it should stop tracking denomination.

The fair summary: Weber’s cultural description, that a religious psychology produced methodical habits which outlived their theology, remains widely influential and useful. His economic claim, that Protestantism was a major cause of capitalist growth, is contested and probably overstated. This site uses the first as a lens and doesn’t depend on the second.
