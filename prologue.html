THE QUESTION UNDER THE QUESTION

Work is where the old devotion went

For many people, work is the last part of modern life that still runs like a religion. It has daily discipline and sacrifice, a feeling of being judged, guilt when you fall short, and the hope of being proven worthy. The vocabulary is economic, but the shape is devotional.

So asking “Why do we call it the Protestant work ethic?” pulls on the one thread still tied to the old spiritual root. Follow it and you hit the question it was built to answer: Am I okay, and how would I know? That question never left. It went underground. And a question that has gone underground comes up the moment you look closely at anything near it.

THE RESTLESS HEART

Why humans ask at all

Three traditions read the hunger behind the question differently. All three agree it’s real.

You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.
Augustine, Confessions I.1 (c. 400)

The religious reading. The restlessness isn’t a malfunction. It’s a homing signal. C. S. Lewis put it as an argument: if we find in ourselves a desire nothing in this world can satisfy, the likeliest explanation is that we were made for another world. Tillich added that seriously asking about what is ultimate already means being grasped by it.

He who has a why to live for can bear with almost any how.
Nietzsche, as quoted by Viktor Frankl in Man’s Search for Meaning

The existential reading. Frankl called it the “will to meaning”: humans need a why the way they need bread. When it’s missing they get what he named the “existential vacuum,” a boredom and emptiness that people try to fill with power, pleasure, or busyness.

Fish don’t notice water.
An old proverb, and a fair summary of Charles Taylor’s argument in A Secular Age (2007)

The historical reading. You can only see a story once you’re partly outside it. For most of history nobody asked “What story are we in?” because the story was simply reality. People ask now because the old answer is no longer taken for granted, so for the first time it’s visible as an answer.

That third reading explains the experience this site is built around: suddenly realizing you’re in the middle of a story you didn’t know was a story. Spiritual traditions have names for that moment, such as awakening and metanoia, the New Testament Greek word usually translated “repentance,” which literally means a turning of the mind.

THE NUMBERS

The question shows up in the data

When an old answer fades and the substitutes don’t hold, it shows up in surveys and even in mortality tables.

ThreadWhat the data shows
BeliefRoughly three in ten American adults now report no religious affiliation, up from under one in ten in the early 1990s (Pew Research Center).
BelongingIn 2020, US church, synagogue, or mosque membership fell below 50% for the first time in Gallup’s eight decades of measuring it.
DespairEconomists Anne Case and Angus Deaton documented rising deaths from drugs, alcohol, and suicide, concentrated among Americans without college degrees. They helped push US life expectancy down three years in a row (2015–2017). They called them “deaths of despair.”
ConnectionIn 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory on “our epidemic of loneliness and isolation.”
BurnoutIn 2019 the World Health Organization added burnout to its International Classification of Diseases as an “occupational phenomenon.”
Counter-signalA 2020 study in JAMA Psychiatry found that US health-care workers who attended religious services weekly had a substantially lower risk of deaths of despair.
A possible turnSome recent surveys report renewed interest in church among young adults, especially young men. Researchers are still debating how large and lasting it is.

Many forces feed these numbers. Taken together, they look like what Part II describes: the search for signs coming up empty.

THE EXCEPTIONS

Who doesn’t suffer this way, and why

Some communities have lived through the same industrial world without it becoming their god.

The Amish are arguably the purest work-ethic culture left in America. They work extremely hard, and many now work in sawmills and factories. But work isn’t where their worth is decided. Their central value is Gelassenheit, “yieldedness”: giving up the self to God and community rather than asserting it. They judge each new tool by one question: does it strengthen or weaken the community? And they choose this life. After a period of adolescent freedom, most choose adult baptism. Studies have found notably lower suicide rates and very little substance abuse.

Durkheim saw this in 1897. His Suicide, one of the founding works of sociology, found that suicide rates tracked social integration. The more tightly a community bound its members (Durkheim compared Protestants, Catholics, and Jews), the more it protected them.

Active Latter-day Saints in a long-term California study by James Enstrom lived roughly a decade longer than the US average. That reflects strong families, dense community, and clear limits on what goes into the body.

What these foundations do

  1. They say your worth isn’t earned by output.Amish yieldedness, Luther’s grace, Tillich’s “you are accepted.” Work matters, but it isn’t the verdict on you.
  2. They hold you in a community.Including when you fail. Durkheim’s integration.
  3. They practice limits.Sabbath and Shabbat, Ramadan and Yom Kippur, dietary law, tools judged by what they do to the community. Regular practices that tell appetite and the market “no” for the sake of something higher.

The Calvinist story adds a warning. A foundation stops protecting when it becomes a test you have to pass. A strong faith that runs on performance can bring the old anxiety right back.

For these communities, industrial work, technology, and the market are things you use, not things you serve. The question of worth is settled somewhere else. That’s where the story begins.
