THE FIRST JOB

Before toil, tending

In Genesis, the first human task comes before any curse: God puts the human in the garden “to dress it and to keep it” (2:15). The Hebrew verbs are ‘avad and shamar, to serve and to guard. ‘Avad is the same root used elsewhere for worship. In the story’s first picture, work and worship aren’t yet separate things.

Toil comes later, after the fall: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (3:19). This distinction matters for everything on this site. The Puritan calling inherited the sweat. The older picture was about tending, a relationship of care with living things.

The image recurs across traditions. “Paradise” comes from the Old Persian pairidaeza, a walled garden. The Qur’an’s paradise is Jannah, literally “the garden.” A well-known hadith tells believers that if the Final Hour arrives while one of them holds a palm seedling, they should still plant it.

FROM ROMANS TO QUAKERS

A household of living things

  • Romans. The household was a religious site. Families kept a lararium, a shrine to the household gods, and made offerings at meals. The hearth belonged to Vesta. Excavations at Pompeii found gardens in house after house, and Pliny the Elder notes that Rome’s urban poor kept window gardens to give their eyes a daily picture of the countryside. Cicero wrote to a friend: “If you have a garden in your library, nothing will be lacking.”
  • Monks. The Benedictine rule, ora et labora (pray and work), put the kitchen garden and the chapel on the same daily schedule. Monasteries kept horticultural knowledge alive through centuries of collapse.
  • Quakers. John Bartram, a Quaker farmer, founded his garden outside Philadelphia in 1728 and became one of colonial America’s great botanists. Bartram’s Garden is still there, the oldest surviving botanic garden in North America.
  • Shakers. Their motto, attributed to founder Ann Lee, was “Hands to work, hearts to God.” They are credited with popularizing garden seeds sold in small paper packets.
  • Even the skeptics. Voltaire ends Candide (1759), his satire of every grand system of meaning, with a garden: “We must cultivate our garden.”
THE HONEST VERSION

What exactly died?

It’s easy to romanticize this, so here is the hard version.

Most people in history weren’t tending pretty gardens. They were doing subsistence farming, which meant toil, hunger, and bad harvests. Around 1790 roughly nine in ten working Americans farmed. Today it’s under two percent. Almost no one wants to go back to that. What people miss is the tending without the toil, which is exactly the line Genesis draws.

So what died isn’t gardening as a hobby. Two older things died:

  1. The household as a center of life. Before industrialization the home produced things: food, cloth, tools, care, and worship. Wage labor moved production out of the house. The home became mainly a place to consume and recover.
  2. Daily obligation to living things. A garden, animals, and a fire all need you every day. They run on seasons you don’t control. They teach patience, attention, and the fact that you aren’t the center. You can’t lie in bed for three days when something alive depends on you.

Then a harder point: belief alone didn’t protect people. Plenty of believers today live the same consuming life as everyone else. What protected the Amish isn’t private faith but practice held in common: the Ordnung, the community’s shared rules of daily life. That’s the lesson of Part I in reverse. The Puritans’ habits outlived their belief. A belief without habits doesn’t outlive much of anything.

THE DEVICE PARADIGM

Focus is Latin for hearth

The philosopher Albert Borgmann (Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life, 1984) gave the clearest account of what replaced tending. His example is heat.

A hearth demands things: someone cuts and stacks wood, someone builds and minds the fire, and the family gathers around it because that’s where the warmth is. Central heating delivers the same warmth with no effort and no gathering. The warmth becomes a commodity, produced out of sight, and the household scatters to separate rooms.

Borgmann calls this the device paradigm: technology increasingly delivers the end product while hiding the work, the skill, and the relationships the end product used to need. His alternative is focal practices, activities that gather people and demand engagement: a family meal, a garden, music, a long walk. He points out that the Latin word for hearth is focus.

Here’s why this belongs in a story about God and the work ethic. The traditions on this page treated tending as sacred. The garden was paradise, the hearth had its goddess, and the kitchen garden was on the monk’s prayer schedule. That kept it protected from convenience. When the sacred faded, tending lost its protection and became just another chore for the device paradigm to remove.

Seeds can stay dormant for a long time. A practice that died in one generation can come back in the next, because the need it met never went away. That’s the seventh way through in Part III.
